ONE MEETING, ONE WHOLE WEEK

One meeting, one whole week.

This is the Acme renewal call — the same fictional sample meeting built into LocalNote, with Maya Chen and Dana Park of Acme. But a meeting is not an hour. It is the week around it: the plan before, the promises after, the chase, the landing. Follow the whole week and you follow the whole product — every feature below is shown at work, in a real moment of that week, because that is the only honest way to show it.

Every name, number and line on this page is fictional — it is the app's own sample data, shown so you can see the work, not just the claims. Nothing here is a real customer or a real call.

The week: Getting ready · Tuesday, the meeting · Tuesday evening · The days after · A week later · Week two · A different Thursday

MONDAY

Getting ready.

The call is on Tuesday. The week starts on Monday — and so does LocalNote.

MONDAY MORNING · THE WEEK TAKES SHAPE

The week is planned around the promises — not over them.

Before he opens anything else, the week is already drafted. LocalNote takes the commitments he is carrying — the Acme checklist due Thursday, the promises still open from earlier meetings — and fits focus blocks around the calendar he already keeps. Proposed, never imposed: nothing is written until he approves the exact blocks.

And if LocalNote could not read a calendar at all, it would draft nothing and say so. Free time is never invented to make a plan look complete.

The brief is a different thing entirely, and it arrives on Tuesday.

The original is never changed until he presses Replace. After he does, the replacement is read back and checked — the words in the app are the words he approved. On the one occasion a paste could not be verified — a PDF form, last month — he was told so, and offered Copy instead.

The last line he does not type at all: he dictates it with ⌘⇧D. And the Acme address he copied before all this? Still on his clipboard afterwards — the clipboard is restored after every paste, dictation included. Later, the same shortcut in his banking app does nothing at all: password managers, banking apps and secure fields are never read. The session remembers a count — three rewrites — and never the text.

A question becomes a case.

The dispute is one sentence long: Acme's finance team says onboarding was billed separately, the way the March agreement had it. His side says Tuesday changed that — and Maya confirmed the change in writing. He opens a ProofDesk case with the question written the way he would have to answer it: Was onboarding billed separately, or included?

Then the exhibits — the records the case will stand on. He attaches the March agreement, Tuesday's meeting record, Maya's confirmed packet and the June email thread. He tries to attach a photo of the whiteboard from the kickoff — and ProofDesk refuses it at the door: exhibits are records, not images, and it says so instead of taking the file and failing later. What is accepted is kept by fingerprint: the exhibit store is content-addressed, every record stored under its own hash, so the same agreement attached twice is stored once — and if a record ever changes, its fingerprint changes with it, and the case knows.

ProofDesk · Chronology (fictional)
MarchThe renewal agreement is signed — onboarding billed separately, in its own words.
JuneThe email thread — the competitor's quote arrives, and the price question starts.
TuesdayThe renewal call — the decision changes: onboarding and migration help included at no extra cost.
A week laterMaya confirms the packet — the new words, bound to version 1 and its fingerprint.

The chronology is assembled from the exhibits themselves. A date the records cannot settle is printed “Date not settled” — never merged into a tidier line than the evidence supports.

WEEK TWO · WHAT THE RECORD SUPPORTS

Findings — with both readings on the table.

ProofDesk reads the exhibits into claims, each one carrying its citation — the exhibit, and the exact place in it. Then it reconciles: the March reading against Tuesday's reading against the confirmed reading, line by line. Where the readings disagree, the case does not pick a winner. It writes a finding — the two readings, and the rule that compared them — and waits for his decision.

ProofDesk · Findings (fictional)
Onboarding: billed, or included?March's words say billed separately. The confirmed packet says included at no extra cost. Both readings are quoted in full; the later, confirmed one is the one he marks as standing.His decision. The case proposed the finding; it did not settle it.
Finance's claim: “the change order was never agreed”Not found in the records selected for this case.That is the strongest sentence ProofDesk will write about an absence — the records he selected do not contain it. Nothing stronger is claimed, and nothing is invented to fill the gap.

Every claim in the case either stands on a citation or is marked as standing on nothing. That is the whole method — and it is why the packet he is about to send can be checked by anyone who receives it.

WEEK TWO · THE PACKET LEAVES

The case, exported — and a key that opens only this case.

He exports the case for Acme's finance team. The preview comes first, and the preview is the file — the exact bytes that will be written, with his redaction rule already applied and the count of redactions on the preview's face. The approval is bound to the packet's digest: if the case changes after the preview, the export refuses and asks for a fresh look, so what he approved is what leaves. He taps Export. Two files land in the folder he chose — the packet, and a manifest that lets the packet be re-verified later. There is no send button. He sends it himself, from his own mail, the way everything leaves.

That evening, his AI assistant app asks what the case concluded. The assistant already holds a broad permission — all his meetings — and under it, it can see no case at all: cases are never inside “all”. Cases have their own door. He grants the cases permission for this one case, read-only — and only then can the assistant list it, and read its claims, its chronology and its standings. Never the exhibits themselves: names, hashes and standings are the whole surface. Every call, and every refusal, is written to the audit log.

Local MCP · Cases scope (fictional)
Under “all meetings”No case is visible. A case is not a meeting, and the broad permission does not reach it.
Under the cases permission — this case onlyClaims, chronology, standings. Read-only. The exhibits stay where they are.
WEEK TWO · BEFORE HE PROMISES

“Can I realistically commit?” — answered before he answers.

Success makes its own demand: Maya asks whether the migration can be delivered in a single week — all twelve seats, finished by Friday of week three. It is exactly the kind of promise that gets made in a good mood and paid for in a bad one. So before he replies, he puts the candidate commitment into the Commitment Firewall, stated as plainly as Maya stated it.

The Firewall reads his real load — the promises already in his ledger, the calendar as it actually stands — and does the arithmetic in the open. The answer is no: the full migration in one week does not fit alongside what he has already promised, and the shortfall is shown, not asserted. And because a bare no is only half an answer, it offers the version that does fit — eight seats that week, the remaining four the week after — with the same honesty about what would have to move to make room.

Commitment Firewall (fictional)
The askFull migration, twelve seats, one week.
The verdictDoes not fit — the week is already promised to the work in the ledger. Shown with its working.
What fitsEight seats that week; four the next. He promises Maya exactly that — the version the arithmetic could stand behind.

Nothing was saved, sent or scheduled by the check itself. It answered a question. The promise he then made was his.

WEEK TWO, EVENING · THE PROPOSAL, CHECKED

Checked against his confirmed records — before it goes out.

The proposal to Acme is a .docx, and it carries the week's promises in writing. Before it goes anywhere, he runs FinalCheck. He selects the records the draft must answer to — his confirmed records: Maya's confirmed packet, the approved project record, the ledger. Nothing is pre-selected for him; the records he confirms are frozen as the authority, hashes and all, and the draft is checked against exactly those.

FinalCheck · Findings (fictional)
Confirmed conflictThe draft bills onboarding separately, at the day rate. The confirmed packet says included at no extra cost. Both sides are quoted exactly — this is a conflict, not a suspicion.
Not found in selected recordsThe draft says Acme's legal team has approved the migration plan. None of the selected records says that. The finding claims nothing more than the absence — and the sentence cannot go out standing on nothing.
Outdated valueThe draft's sign-by date is the one from the proposal's first version — a date that stands in an older draft, since replaced by the Friday Maya confirmed.
Unable to determineThe draft promises migration “by Wednesday”. His records disagree about which Wednesday was agreed — so the checker cites both sides and refuses to pick. No repair is offered where the record itself is unsettled; that one is his to settle.

Where a single exact replacement exists, a minimal repair is proposed — the confirmed words substituted for the draft's, never a recomputed figure, never a rewritten sentence. He approves each repair on its own; every approval is bound to the hash of the draft and the hashes of the records, so if either changes, the approval goes stale instead of going through. And because the draft is a .docx, the repairs land as a repaired copy — the original file is never edited. Read-back then checks the copy, and reports in its three words: verified — the approved words are there, and nothing else moved.

The check's report lists what was examined, what was found and what he decided. One word never appears in it: it does not call the proposal correct. Correct is a claim about the world; the report only claims what the records support.

FRIDAY, WEEK TWO · TIME, KEPT HONESTLY

Time comes back — and the ledger refuses to exaggerate it.

TimeBack has been watching the shape of his weeks — his calendar against his commitments — and it proposes one opportunity: the standing Friday status call with Acme. The week's records show its business already done in the thread; the call is a habit, not a need. The proposal waits in the queue as a proposal — an estimate, labelled as one — until he approves it.

He approves it. TimeBack applies the change through the calendar, and then reads the calendar back. Only the read-back earns the ledger its one strong sentence:

TimeBack Ledger (fictional)
Calendar time freedThe Friday status block is gone from the calendar — verified against the calendar itself, not against the intention.
On his wordA focus block he moved himself, and says saved him time. Recorded as his attestation, in its own column — never upgraded, never mixed into the freed time.
A schedule changeAnother block that refilled with different work. Counted as a change of shape — not as time regained.

Three kinds of “time back”, kept in three separate columns, because they are three different kinds of true. This page will not total them into a savings figure — the ledger's job is to keep them honest, and it does it by refusing to blur them.

A different Thursday.

THURSDAY · THE SCHOOL MEETING

What the school promised, in minutes — against what arrived.

Her child's plan — the IEP — promises speech therapy in writing: 120 minutes a week. Promises made to a school do not live in a ledger of dollars; they live in minutes, and minutes are exactly what the IEP Ledger lines up: what the plan promised, against what was actually delivered, week by week.
