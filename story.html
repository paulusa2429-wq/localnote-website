SUNDAY TO FRIDAY · ONE CONNECTED WEEK

One week. One thread.

One week in one consultant's work — Sunday night to Friday evening — and one client: the Acme renewal, the same fictional sample meeting built into LocalNote, with Maya Chen and Dana Park of Acme. Nothing here resets: Sunday's spoken note is pinned to Monday's screen; Monday's brief is practised, then carried into Tuesday's room; Tuesday's promises are chased on Wednesday, disputed on Thursday, and landed on Friday. Follow the thread and you follow the whole product — every feature is at work inside the story, because that is the only honest way to show it.

Every name, number and line on this page is fictional — it is the app's own sample data, shown so you can see the work, not just the claims. Nothing here is a real customer or a real call.

The week: Sunday night · Monday · Tuesday · Wednesday · Thursday · Friday · Friday evening

SUNDAY, 21:40 · THE WEEK, SPOKEN

He says the week out loud, and it stays said.

Sunday night, he thinks better out loud. He opens the voice inbox and speaks the week the way he wants it to go:

Voice inbox · Sunday 21:40
Spoken note, 40 seconds“Tuesday is the Acme renewal. Settle the seats once and for all. Don't promise migration dates until somebody on their side names an owner. And Thursday, two o'clock, is my daughter's school review — that does not move, whatever the week does.”

One line he wants in tomorrow's brief, so he dictates it instead of typing: ⌘⇧D — “Ask Dana who owns the migration timeline on their side — nobody has ever actually said.” Nothing about Sunday night is a meeting. All of it becomes Monday's inputs.

MONDAY MORNING · MY DAY

My Day. His job is a short queue of approvals.

Monday opens on the queue. Behind it, the jobs engine ran overnight — its work survives restarts, never runs twice, and prepares rather than acts. The automatic backup ran at 6:12; the one notification LocalNote allows itself all day has already said, in generic words, that things are waiting — the details live here. This morning's digest lays out the day ahead, each event with its brief built for it. And pinned at the top: Sunday's spoken intent. Nothing in the queue has been sent, changed or decided. His first approval is the plan.

MONDAY · THE PLAN, THEN THE BRIEF

The week is planned around the promises — then the record is opened.

The Day Planner takes the commitments he is carrying — open promises from earlier meetings, the Acme checklist due Thursday — and fits focus blocks around the calendar he already keeps, and around the one block Sunday's note marked immovable. Proposed, never imposed: he approves the exact blocks, they are written to his Mac calendar and read back so he knows they landed. With no calendar readable, it would draft nothing and say so, rather than invent free time.

The blocks: Tue 9:00, prepare for the Acme call · Thu 10:00, send the onboarding checklist · Thu 14:00, his daughter's school review — immovable, as Sunday asked · Fri 16:30, close the week. He approves them; the read-back receipt says Verified — the blocks are in the calendar itself. (They could have gone out as a calendar file instead.)

Plan approved, he opens the brief — built from everything LocalNote holds about Acme: the work memory of the March agreement, the kickoff call, the people. One thing it leans on is not a meeting at all. He presses ⌘K and searches the whole record at once — “renewal cap” — and lands in his own mail archive: .eml messages and an .mbox archive, imported as files. Import only — no account connects, the search works with the Wi-Fi off, and the mail arrives as plain text, so nothing buried in HTML mail can ever run.

Brief · Acme renewal call
From the June thread (email archive)Dana Park: “Legal needs three weeks for a full review of any renewal terms… finance is budgeting ten seats, not twelve — still unsettled.” And: “Finance capped the renewal at last year's figure.” Her page adds her open promise to you — confirm the final seat count, still open from the kickoff.

The brief carries his saved questions for Tuesday — Sunday's dictated line first: who owns the migration timeline on Acme's side? Then: the seats — finance's ten, or the room's twelve? And legal's three weeks, against a Friday signature? And it names the decision on record, the one this meeting will be checked against: the March agreement — onboarding billed separately, at the standard day rate.

MONDAY · PRACTISE, WRITE, SWITCH ON

He practises the hard conversation before he has it.

The brief's saved questions become a script. The price question is coming — Dana has been circling it since June — so he opens Rehearsal mode: a practise run walled off from the record, a stand-in Dana built from his past meetings with her, pushing the way she pushes. Nothing a stand-in says is evidence of anything. It is sparring, so he can be ready.

The stand-in opens where Dana always opens: “Four thousand is more than we budgeted. You know we have another quote.” He parries the way he means to on Tuesday: “I do. What if the number stays — and the onboarding and migration help come with it?”

Her last line is the gap: “Then I'd ask what the onboarding is worth on its own… and the revised quote — that comes from you, I assume?” He thought March had settled who sends the revised quote — but thought is not a citation, so he asks the record, across all his meetings. The answer comes back verified, its source quoted word for word: Dana Park. The March agreement names her as the sender of the revised quote. An answer that cannot show its source is not given as one.

The brief's last page is a prep email. In his mail app, one paragraph is clumsy; he selects it, presses ⌘⇧E, and the Writing Assistant rewrites it where it was written — “Ahead of Tuesday's call: we propose renewing at last year's terms, and we will walk through the onboarding together.” The original is never changed until he presses Replace; afterwards the replacement is read back and checked. (In his banking app, later, the same shortcut does nothing at all — secure fields are never read — and the session remembers a count of rewrites, never the text.) He sends the email himself.

One more thing before Monday ends. Last quarter, a dispute with a different client turned on how finance had worded a claim, and he kept the case. He reaches for it now — and gets not an error, but a popup: ProofDesk is off. In Settings, the pending list names every feature still switched off. He turns on the three this kind of week tends to need — ProofDesk, TimeBack, FinalCheck — one tap each, and leaves the rest off. He does not know yet that all three will fire before Friday.

TUESDAY, 10:00 · AT ACME'S OFFICE

Recorded on this computer. No bot in the room.

As he walks in, a card is already floating over his other apps: LocalNote has noticed the calendar event, and offers — in one line, with the brief it prepared — “Acme renewal, 10:00. Where you left off: the seats, unsettled since June.” He taps Start notes. His laptop on the table, in-person mode, one button. The consent reminder is shown to the room and written to the encrypted, hash-chained log. Before anyone speaks, he sets the meeting's language once — the Hindi–English mix this room talks in — and it holds all meeting. A name appears next to each voice as they speak:

Transcript · Acme renewal call (excerpt)
0:18 · Maya Chen“Thanks. Our current plan renews on the 30th, and we want to keep the same seats — twelve in total.”
1:15 · Dana Park“Four thousand is more than we budgeted. A competitor quoted us thirty-two hundred for the same seats.”
2:15 · You“I understand. What if we keep the price at four thousand and include the onboarding and the migration help at no extra cost?”
4:45 · Maya Chen“Decision then: we renew at four thousand, with onboarding included, and we sign by Friday.”

At 1:15, as Dana names the competitor's price, a hint surfaces at the edge of his screen — Live Assist, reading the live transcript against his past meetings with these people: June, Dana's own words: finance capped the renewal at last year's figure — and the competitor's $3,200 is for the seats alone. Shown once, quietly, never written into the notes; he doesn't read it aloud, he just doesn't get cornered. Four minutes in, Maya's colleague from finance hurries in late; the consent reminder is shown again for the late joiner, and the log notes both arrivals.

Not everything arrives clean, and nothing unclean is hidden. The transcription flags one word it was unsure of — “migration” — instead of guessing: he taps the flag, hears the moment again, confirms it. The first pass wrote Dana's surname as “Parke”; he teaches it once — Park — and it is fixed in this meeting and in the kickoff's, everywhere (his jargon and Acme's glossary ride along). One line he corrects by hand; the edit is marked as his. Five minutes in, his laptop battery gives out — and the recording is intact to the last second before it died. It was never lost.

Afterwards, in the corridor, Maya warns him: finance will ask about the onboarding — they always do. He records it on his phone and imports the file; its speakers will be labelled overnight. On the stairs he dictates two lines into the record: “Finance will push on onboarding — Maya, corridor.” And: “Migration owner, their side — still unnamed.” Sunday's question is still open; the week is keeping it for him.

TUESDAY, AFTER · THE CHECK

The summary is checked against what was actually said.

Back at his desk, the summary — shaped, for a call like this, by the sales template he picked for it — is matched line by line against the transcript. Anything the meeting never backed is stamped in the open, never quietly dropped or kept. One line is backed, word for word — the renewal at $4,000, twelve seats, onboarding included, signature by Friday:

Summary · Acme renewal call, checked
UNCONFIRMED“Acme agreed to match the competitor's $3,200 price.”Nobody agreed to that.
UNCONFIRMED“Legal has approved the renewal terms.”The meeting said the opposite: legal still has to review them, and Dana will confirm by Wednesday.

For Maya's colleague in Madrid, the summary switches to Spanish — summaries come in English, Spanish and Hindi — and where it quotes the call, the translation sits under the original words, never instead of them: “We renew at four thousand, with onboarding included, and we sign by Friday.” — “Renovamos por cuatro mil, con la incorporación incluida, y firmamos el viernes.” The original is the record; the translation is only a guide. (On her screen the app itself is in Spanish — its interface speaks 32 languages; a meeting's language is a separate choice, made once.)

Then the signed paper is set against the room — the March agreement, the same SOW the June thread carried. The price: agreed. Onboarding: contradicted — the paper bills it separately; the room, at 2:15, included it at no extra cost. The price-review clause: never mentioned, and reported as exactly that. And the seat count — Monday's second saved question — does not survive either: Dana's June words had finance budgeting ten seats, unsettled; the room stated twelve and decided twelve. The Contradiction Detector fires, and he saves its receipt — both sides in their exact words, one document he can produce whenever the question is asked. The disagreement itself is the document.

The rest of the record's flags wait in My Day under “Needs review”, raised by Work Guardian and settled by nobody but him: an owner conflict (March names Dana as sender of the revised quote — Monday's verified answer said the same — but the room put it on Maya); a dependency risk (Friday's signature waits on legal's review, due Wednesday, unconfirmed); and the approval the summary claimed, answered exactly, never stronger than the evidence — “Approval not found in selected records.” Friday will close these. First, the promises. This is the same check you can run on this site: run it on a summary of your own →

TUESDAY, AFTER · THE PROMISES

Every promise lands in one ledger.

The meeting made four promises. None lives in someone's memory or a notebook margin — each is a ledger row, with its owner, its deadline, and the exact words it came from:

Commitments · Acme renewal call
Send the revised renewal quote — Maya Chen · by Friday · tracked
Confirm the legal review of the renewal terms — Dana Park · by Wednesday, 9:00 · tracked
Send the onboarding checklist — You · by Thursday · tracked
Schedule the kickoff — Maya Chen · by next Monday · tracked

Open any promise and its whole life is there — an evidence timeline. Dana's starts before the promise does (June: “Legal needs three weeks for a full review”), takes in Tuesday's exact words — “I'll confirm their review by Wednesday” — and notes the reminder set from the ledger for Wednesday 9:00 — and that Friday's signature depends on this one. Reminders are set for all four. The ledger is just as honest about the promise this meeting closed: Dana's seat-count confirmation, from the kickoff, fell due last week and left no trace. The Slip Detector closes it in plain words — a promise that slipped, its evidence attached: the answer arrived in the room at 0:18, but not by the road, or the date, that was promised.

One promise of his own he can finish today, so he does: the seat plan Maya asked for, sent within the hour and marked done in the ledger with a single tap — the sent file attached, because here “done” is a claim that carries its evidence. Before dinner, the day's promises come back as three quick questions in the Commitment Quiz — who confirms legal's review, and by when? what did the room change about onboarding? whose promise slipped? Thirty seconds. The ledger keeps the promises; the quiz keeps them fresh.

TUESDAY EVENING · THE FOLLOW-UP, READY

The follow-up is written. It is not sent.

By the time he is home, a follow-up draft is waiting in his queue, prepared from the meeting itself. But before a draft may wait for him, it is checked: Border Guard reads outgoing drafts, and this one does not pass clean. Its first version repeated the summary's invented line — that legal had approved the terms. The claim the check stamped UNCONFIRMED at noon tries to leave the building at six; the line is flagged with the transcript moment that contradicts it, and the draft cannot be marked ready until he strikes it. He strikes it.

Then he runs the two recipes he saved for exactly this — his own prompts, kept in the app, run over this one meeting. The first turns the meeting into the client email, in the format he taught it; the second turns the same notes into the task list for the project file — quote, legal review, checklist, kickoff, each with its owner and its day. Same record, chosen formats, answers with their sources attached:

Recipes · Run on the Acme renewal call
Recipe: “Client follow-up” → the email“Hi Maya, Dana — confirming what we decided today: Acme renews at $4,000 a year for twelve seats, onboarding and migration help included at no extra cost, signature by Friday.” — and the next steps, each with its owner and its day.

The email is a touch long, so he polishes it — one tap on Shorter — and the rewrite comes back as a candidate only; the original stands until he taps “Use this version”, which he does. Work Mode makes the meeting into the status document the project file needs; the brief goes out as a PDF for that file, and the notes themselves as a Markdown file beside it — exported to his files, uploaded nowhere. He saves the 4:45 decision as a highlight clip — twelve seconds of Maya's own voice. Then he approves the follow-up, and it goes — by his hand, like everything that ever leaves.

WEDNESDAY · THE QUEUE, THE GOAL, THE CRACK

The Closer has done the only thing it ever does: prepared, and waited.

Wednesday's Approve-All queue is built entirely from Tuesday's ledger. Its top item is Dana's promise — due at nine, unlanded by half past; the Overdue Nudge prepared the chase overnight, built from the ledger entry and its evidence timeline:

The chase draft waits with it — “Hi Dana — a gentle nudge on legal's review of the renewal terms, promised for Wednesday. The signature is set for Friday, so today would really help. Anything I can unblock?” — with three ways to answer it: approve and send, edit first, or leave it. He approves it.

The same queue holds Tuesday's corridor recording, its speakers labelled while he slept — Maya's warning now in the record, in her own voice: finance will ask about the onboarding. And a smaller proposal rides along: the app suggests filing Tuesday's meeting under “Acme”. It never files anything itself — he confirms, and only then does the meeting move.

Wednesday afternoon he stops treating the renewal as a meeting and sets it as a goal — close the Acme renewal by month-end. The Goal Agent plans the steps deterministically and prepares each one for his approval; approval is not a rubber stamp here, approval is the work. Step 1, the decision packet — Tuesday's decision in its exact words, in a file Maya can answer without installing anything — he approves, reads the invitation drafted for his own mail app (LocalNote sends nothing itself), and presses send. Approved deliverables are saved to the folder he chose. The agent does not send, book or decide.

But the day's real news is quieter. In March he pinned an assumption — “Legal has approved the renewal terms” — and the Assumption Sentinel has been watching it ever since. Against the record, it does not hold: Tuesday, Dana said legal still has to review them; the check stamped the same claim UNCONFIRMED; Wednesday's confirmation is overdue and the chase is already out. Both sides are shown — what he pinned, and what the week did to it. By evening the crack has a voice: Maya forwards finance's email. They are holding the March line — onboarding billed separately — and, as far as their records go, the change he is relying on “was never agreed”. Tomorrow it stops being an email. Tomorrow it becomes a case.

THURSDAY MORNING · A QUESTION BECOMES A CASE

First, his own Thursday promise. Then, the case.

Thursday opens with his own ledger entry — the onboarding checklist, promised Tuesday, due today. It goes out in the morning, marked done with the sent file attached. Then Maya's answer to Wednesday's packet lands: she confirms the decision in its exact words, bound to the packet's version and its fingerprint — and her confirmation becomes the case's fourth exhibit. The question is finance's: was onboarding billed separately, or included? ProofDesk — switched on Monday, before he knew he would need it — opens the case, and its exhibits are artefacts this story has already produced: the March agreement, Tuesday's record, the June thread, and Maya's confirmed packet. A photograph of the whiteboard is refused at attach: exhibits are records, not images. The store is content-addressed: a duplicate is stored once, and a changed record's fingerprint changes with it.

ProofDesk · The onboarding question
Findings — both readings on the tableMarch says billed separately; Tuesday says included at no extra cost; Maya's confirmed words side with Tuesday. The finding shows both readings and the rule that compared them, and waits for his marking of which stands. Finance's claim — that the change “was never agreed” — gets the pinned wording, and nothing stronger: Not found in the records selected for this case.

The chronology assembles itself from the exhibits — March, the agreement signed; June, the thread; Tuesday, the room deciding otherwise — and one date no record settles is printed as it stands: Date not settled, never quietly merged. Before anything leaves, the Privacy Vault does its work: the export is checked against the consent log — Tuesday's consent, the late joiner's — and redaction applied, its count on the preview's face. The preview is the file; the approval is bound to the packet's digest, so a changed case refuses it; and there is no send button. He sends it himself. One door opens, carefully: his own AI assistant needs the case's standings, so he grants it the separate cases scope — for this one case, read-only. Under the broad “all meetings” scope it sees no case at all; under this grant it reads the claims, the chronology and the standings — never a record's bytes. Every call, and every refusal, is written to the audit log. The afternoon is not Acme's at all.

THURSDAY, 14:00 · THE SCHOOL

What the school promised, in minutes — against what the weeks delivered.

At two, the block the Planner set on Monday — the one Sunday's voice note marked immovable — takes him across town, and the reminder the ledger set brings the file with him. His daughter's plan promises 120 minutes a week of support. Her IEP Ledger sets the promise against the record, week by week: 90 minutes delivered in the first week, 60 in the second — and the third week says, in plain words, Not recorded. Never zero: an empty week is a gap in the record, not a fact about his daughter. The evidence pack he hands the advocate is the ProofDesk habit in his own family's life — the same product, the same man, the same week. Two new commitments enter his ledger before he leaves: the school's, and his own. Friday will have to fit around both.

FRIDAY · BEFORE HE PROMISES

“Can I realistically commit?” — answered before he answers.

Friday opens with one less unknown: Dana's confirmation landed late Thursday — legal's review complete, two days after it was promised. The ledger closes her promise as what it was: kept, late, the lateness on the record. Then Maya asks for the thing he has been dreading politely all week: the full migration, in one week. Before he answers, TimeBack's Commitment Firewall checks the candidate against his real load — the ledger's open promises, the Planner's calendar, Thursday's school commitments now sitting in both — and answers honestly, with the working shown: no. Not that week. What fits is eight seats that week and four the next, and that — only that — is what he promises Maya. The new promise lands in the same ledger as Tuesday's four. The check itself saved nothing, sent nothing, scheduled nothing; it just refused to let him lie to a client by accident.

FRIDAY · CHECKED BEFORE IT GOES

The proposal is checked against his confirmed records — before it goes anywhere.

The promise needs a proposal — a .docx carrying the week's promises in writing. FinalCheck — the third switch he turned on Monday — runs it against his confirmed records only: the case findings, the ledger, the March agreement. Nothing is pre-selected; the set is frozen, hashes and all. It finds a confirmed conflict (the draft bills onboarding separately; the confirmed words say included), a claim not found in selected records (the draft's legal approval), an outdated value (the first version's sign-by date), and one point it marks unable to determine — two records disagree, both are cited, no repair offered for that one. The repairs it does offer are exact substitutions of the confirmed words, approved one by one, each bound to the hashes of draft and source. Because the draft is a .docx, they land as a repaired copy — the original is never edited, and the check says so. The copy is read back and verified; the report, pointedly, never calls the proposal “correct”. He sends it himself.

With the proposal sent, the standing Friday status call has nothing left to do — its business is done, in writing, in the thread. A TimeBack opportunity is proposed as what it is, a labelled estimate; he approves it; the cancellation is applied through the calendar and read back — and only then does the ledger use the words Calendar time freed, the wording reserved for a verified change. A block he moved himself sits in its own column as his attestation, never upgraded, never mixed in; a block that refilled is a schedule change, not regained time. The ledger refuses to total the columns into a savings figure. It keeps the two kinds of truth apart, the way the whole week has.

FRIDAY · THE LANDING

Approved work lands in the file — and is read back.

The Acme project keeps its own task file, and Friday is when the week's promises meet it. Project Sync proposes the ledger's promises as rows — exact matches only, anything uncertain held back for his eyes — and each approved landing is read back from the file itself, with a receipt in one of three honest words: Verified, Failed, Uncertain. One approval has gone stale — the content changed underneath it — and is refused until he looks again. One action he cancels; the emergency stop is there, and shown, for the day everything needs to halt at once. The consultant Profession Pack he works under shapes the change order the sync produces, and the Revenue Pack prices it from the same record — marked, in its own words, not billable until approved. Underneath, the completion ledger keeps the week's two kinds of done apart: verified by a read-back, and standing on his word.

The change order goes out the way Wednesday's packet went — one small file, an invitation drafted for his own mail app — and Maya answers it: confirmed, bound to version 1 and its fingerprint, the identity caveat stated as plainly as on Thursday. Her confirmation proposes the record update, and Work Guardian closes the loop the check opened on Tuesday. The confirmed words are set against the March baseline — never edited, and not edited now. Tuesday's scope mismatch is resolved on the evidence the week produced; the correction lands as a guarded file of its own, and the approval state is marked by him, by hand. The flags that waited in Needs review all week are settled — not by the app, but by the person the record belongs to.

FRIDAY EVENING · AT HOME, ON WINDOWS

Home. The receipts for the week.

Friday evening he is on the Windows machine at home, in the hour the status call used to take. The same record opens — same files, same ledger; nothing about the week lives in anyone's cloud. The jobs engine that opened his Monday has also closed his Friday: the week's recap is written and waiting. He exports the connection log as an audit report — every place the app reached all week, what for, and how much went each way; Thursday's MCP grant is in it, calls and refusals alike — so “nothing leaves this computer” is something he can check, not something he is asked to believe. The partner copy of the case goes out redacted, the way Thursday's was. He sets the week's recordings to delete themselves when their retention runs out; the usage counts stay off — counting is opt-in, and he has not opted in. One thing is left: a problem report about Tuesday's flagged word, which he reads line by line — it says what will leave, and nothing else leaves with it — and sends himself. The week is on the record. And the record is his.
